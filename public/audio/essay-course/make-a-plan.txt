Lesson three. Make a plan before you write.

Many students feel that planning wastes time, especially in an exam. The book disagrees strongly. A little thought, turned into brief notes, pays off in relevance, order and clarity.

Think about everyday life. When a friend asks your opinion, your answer sounds much better when you think for a moment before you jump in with both feet. Essays work the same way.

A plan helps two people. It helps you, because a clear shape in your head makes the writing easier. And it helps your reader, the teacher or examiner. The book says meet the assessors more than halfway. Make their job easy, and they will be on your side.

Every essay has three parts. A definite opening, a considered development, and an emphatic conclusion. The middle part, the body, is by far the longest. But the opening and the ending matter just as much.

So how do you plan? First, read the title and underline the key words. Then jot down quick words and phrases that come to mind. They can be messy and random at first. Next, keep only what is relevant to the question. Finally, put what is left in order. What goes in the opening? Which points make the body, one for each paragraph? And what will you say at the end?

How long should this take? The book says no more than five to seven minutes when you have an hour or less for the essay. With a shorter essay, keep it shorter still. Do not get so carried away with notes that you run out of time to write.

And remember this. An exam is not a race to fill the most pages. As an examiner, the author sometimes had to put a line through page after page of answers that were simply not relevant. Quality beats length every time.

Your turn. Take the book's title about the internet being a very mixed blessing, and write a short plan. One line for the opening, three or four points for the body, and one line for the conclusion.
