Lesson six. One point per paragraph, with a key sentence.

You made a promise in your opening. Now you have to come through with the goods. The body of the essay is where you earn most of your marks.

Paragraphs are your best friends here. Imagine a page with no breaks at all. It is tiring to read, and the ideas seem jumbled. Paragraphs guide your reader from one idea to the next in a clear order.

The book gives a simple rule. Deal with one main point in each paragraph. If you pack three or four points into one paragraph, your reader gets confused, and you end up treating each point only on the surface.

How do you show the reader what the point is? With a key sentence. The book says it gives the reader a key to unlock what the paragraph is about. Put it first, and your reader knows at once where you are going.

Here is the book's example. A paragraph about celebrity culture begins with this key sentence. Celebrity culture, then, is a well established feature of our mass media. Everything else in the paragraph supports that one idea. Programmes about celebrities appear on television every day. Producers have learnt that the word celebrity brings in more viewers.

Now picture a weak paragraph on the same topic. It talks about celebrities on television, then jumps to fashion, then to the internet, then to young people and their dreams, all in a few lines. There is no key sentence, and no point is developed. The reader is left asking, what is this paragraph really about?

The book says the more signals like this you give, the more your essay hangs together. One point. One key sentence. Placed at the start.

Your turn. Take the title about the internet being a very mixed blessing. Choose one main point for a body paragraph and write a key sentence for it. Then list the details you would use to support it.
