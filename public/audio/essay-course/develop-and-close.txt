Lesson seven. Develop the point and close the paragraph.

A key sentence on its own is not enough. You must give it flesh, as the book says. That means examples, details and explanation.

The book shows a neat shape for a body paragraph. First the key sentence. Then a few sentences that develop it with specific examples. Then a closing sentence that rounds the paragraph off.

Let us look at the celebrity paragraph again. After the key sentence, the next line gives an example. Programmes about celebrities appear daily on television. Then come more details, about celebrities sent off to jungles and set strange tests for our entertainment, and producers chasing higher viewing figures.

Then comes the close. It begins with the word Thus. Thus, we the viewers play our part, because we give the producers the audience they need. The book calls this an intermediate conclusion. It is a small conclusion drawn from the evidence in that one paragraph.

The very last sentence can also point the way to the next paragraph. If we did not watch these programmes, they would soon stop being made. Now the next paragraph can pick up the idea of audience figures and carry it forward.

Another example in the book is about charity concerts for famine relief. The key sentence says that perhaps nothing has really changed since the first concert. The next sentences give the evidence. Famine, drought, disease and civil war remain. The closing sentence says that many aid workers now look to other solutions, which leads neatly into the next paragraph.

So remember the pattern. Key sentence. Development with real examples. Closing sentence that sums up or looks ahead.

Your turn. Write one full body paragraph. Start with a key sentence, add three sentences that develop it with specific examples, and finish with a closing sentence. You can use the internet title or the voting age title.
