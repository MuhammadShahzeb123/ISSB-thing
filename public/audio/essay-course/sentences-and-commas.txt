Lesson thirteen. Sentences and commas.

A well planned essay can still lose marks through careless mistakes. The book admits that anyone can make an error, especially when writing fast. The trick is to keep them few. An occasional slip can be overlooked, but a page full of them will cost you.

Start with complete sentences. A complete sentence makes sense on its own. The country declared war on the day after the invasion. That is complete. Because the voters turned against the party. That is not. It leaves you waiting for the rest. If you are unsure, read the sentence in your head and ask whether it makes sense alone.

Next, mix your sentence lengths. A short, simple sentence makes an emphatic point. Africa is facing a famine crisis. A longer, complex sentence lets you explain and analyse in detail. Good essays use both.

What you should avoid is a long chain of short sentences. The book shows a paragraph about fashion models made of seven short sentences. Models only parade on a catwalk. They wear designer clothes. Yet they earn huge sums of money. It sounds choppy and monotonous. Joined into three sentences, with words like because and which, it flows and sounds more thoughtful.

Now full stops. Some students join whole sentences with commas. The football authorities are very concerned about agents, they are seen to be profiting from the game. That comma is not strong enough. It needs a full stop, and a capital letter to start the next sentence.

Linking words such as however, of course and nevertheless usually take commas. At the start of a sentence, put a comma after them. In the middle of a sentence, put one before and one after.

Finally, beware of what the book calls comma itis, a rash of commas scattered everywhere. The rock star, reacted angrily, to the questions. Those commas do not belong. Use a comma only where the meaning needs it.

Your turn. You will see a short passage with sentences run together and too many commas. Rewrite it with correct full stops and commas.
