Lesson five. Build your opening paragraph.

You have a strong first sentence. What comes next? The book gives a clear guide. Aim for an opening paragraph of four or five sentences.

The job of this paragraph is not to answer the whole question. Its job is to map out the ground. You tell the reader what you are going to do. Then in the body you keep your word. And then you round it all off in the conclusion.

Let us walk through an example from the book. The title asks whether the voting age should be lowered to sixteen, and asks you to weigh up the arguments for and against.

Sentence one goes straight to the topic. Debate about the voting age usually centres on whether young people are mature enough to make a reasoned judgement about their vote.

Sentence two follows on. It asks whether most voters of any age really vote with mature judgement, or simply out of habit or prejudice.

Sentence three pushes the idea further. Perhaps maturity is not the real issue at all.

Sentence four opens the next door. More central to the debate may be the rights of every citizen in a democracy, whatever their age.

Notice what happened. Each sentence grows out of the one before it. And each one is a small promise. Maturity, the habits of voters, and the rights of citizens. The reader now expects all three to come back later in the essay.

The book also offers some useful starting phrases. In this essay I intend to explore. This essay will discuss. This essay will focus on. You do not have to use them, but they give a brisk and direct start.

Before you move on, test your opening with the book's own questions. Does it deal with the topic at once? Is it specific? Does it map out what comes next? Does it avoid waffle?
