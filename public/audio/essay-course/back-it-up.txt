Lesson nine. Back up every claim.

A statement without evidence is only an assertion. The book says that whatever the subject, assertions must be matched with detail and specific references.

In a literature essay this evidence is called a close reference. It might be a key event in the story, something a character says or does, or a short quotation. The book compares this to a lawyer in court. A good lawyer does not just say that someone is guilty. The lawyer shows the evidence.

Here is the book's example from the novel Pride and Prejudice. The key sentence says that Elizabeth has plenty of reason to think Darcy is proud. Then come three brief references. He refuses to dance with her at the ball. He looks down on her mother. And his marriage proposal suggests he had to overcome his doubts about her lower social position. Three short pieces of evidence, and the claim stands up.

Notice what the writer did not do. They did not retell the whole story. The book is very clear about this. Close references are not the same as telling the story in your own words. Pick a few sharp details that prove your point, and move on.

The same rule works for history and politics. In an essay on why the Labour Party won the British election of nineteen forty five, the writer does not just say the nineteen thirties were hard. They name mass unemployment, poor housing, hunger and the lack of free medical care. That is real evidence.

Quotations should be short and relevant. And use the proper terms of your subject, without showing off with jargon for its own sake.

For your ISSB essay this means real names, places, dates and examples, instead of vague words like many people say or some experts believe.

Your turn. You will see a bare claim. Rewrite it as a short paragraph that backs it up with at least two specific pieces of evidence, without telling a long story.
