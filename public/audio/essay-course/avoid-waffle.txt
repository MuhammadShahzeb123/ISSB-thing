Lesson four. Start strong and avoid waffle.

Think about picking up a book in a shop. You read the first paragraph, and that paragraph decides whether you keep reading. Your examiner has to read your essay anyway, but your first paragraph still shapes how they feel about everything that follows. You want a happy reader, not a tired one.

The biggest enemy of a good opening is waffle. Waffle is empty, general talk that could fit almost any essay. Students use it to hide the fact that they have nothing much to say yet.

Here is the kind of opening the book warns against. This is a very important issue and there are many different approaches that can be taken. Many experts have considered this matter. There are arguments for and against, and many people feel very strongly about it.

What is the topic? You cannot tell. The book points out that a paragraph like that says nothing in forty five words. Another favourite is the line that a question has perplexed historians down through the years. It sounds serious, but again it says nothing at all.

So what should you do instead? Deal with the topic from the very first sentence. A useful trick is to use some of the key words of the title in your opening line.

The book shows this with the novel Great Expectations. The question says that Pip loses his moral values and has to win them back. A strong first sentence names Pip, names his values, such as kindness and hard work, and says where he lost them, when he left for London. In one sentence the examiner knows you are answering the real question.

Be specific, but do not try to pack everything in. The detail belongs in the body. The opening simply shows that you are on the ball from the very first line.

Your turn. You will see a waffly first sentence for a title about celebrity culture. Rewrite it as one specific sentence that uses the key words of the title.
