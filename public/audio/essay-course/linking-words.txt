Lesson eight. Link your paragraphs.

Your reader must be able to follow the flow of your essay. The book wants every essay to feel like one connected whole, with each section fitting neatly into the one before.

The tool for this is linking words and phrases. They are signposts. They tell your reader where your argument is going, and they show the examiner that you are in control.

Here are some that the book suggests. However. Nevertheless. Furthermore. Moreover. On the contrary. Secondly. Therefore. Thus. Finally. And longer ones, such as another essential feature of, or to counter this argument, or the bulk of the available evidence, then, points to.

The book shows them at work in an essay on climate change. The first paragraph says that experts may disagree on the causes, but hardly anyone denies that the weather is changing. The second paragraph opens with However, and explains how selfish national interests get in the way. The third opens with Nevertheless, and turns to the progress that has been made. Three small words, and the reader never gets lost.

Linking words do not always have to come first. In one example about sport, the word however is the eighth word of the paragraph. That is fine, as long as it sits in the first sentence. Notice too that when however comes in the middle of a sentence, it needs a comma before it and after it.

Small words like this and that can also link ideas. A paragraph that talks about this obsession is pointing back to the paragraph before it.

Now picture an essay with no links at all. It jumps from point to point, and the examiner has to work out the connections alone. The book says that is exactly what examiners do not want to read.

Your turn. You will see the first sentences of three body paragraphs with no links. Rewrite each one so that it carries a suitable linking word or phrase.
