Lesson fourteen. Apostrophes and spelling.

Some small errors annoy examiners more than you would expect. The apostrophe is near the top of the list.

Begin with it's and its. The one with an apostrophe means it is. The one without means belonging to it. The book shows two newspaper headlines that get it wrong. A headline that shouts its a record needs an apostrophe, because it means it is a record. A headline about a car that says it's wheels came off should not have one, because the wheels belong to it. The book calls mixing these up a bad error.

Apostrophes have two main jobs. They show that something belongs to someone, as in my daughter's education or the school's reputation. And they show that letters are missing, as in don't, can't and won't.

Possessive words such as yours, ours, hers and theirs never take an apostrophe.

Here is another common mistake. Never use an apostrophe just to make a plural. Wild animals deserve to be protected from poachers. No apostrophes there at all, because nothing belongs to anything. Only when a plural owns something does it take one, as in politicians' instincts or women's rights.

Now spelling. The book picks out some mix ups that make a poor impression. Their means belonging to them, as in their house. There shows that something exists, or means in that place, as in there is no doubt, or over there. They're is short for they are.

The same goes for were, where and we're. Were is a part of the verb to be. Where asks about or points to a place. We're is short for we are.

The book also gives a long list of words you will use often in essays, such as necessary, argument, government and environment. Learn to spell them. And in coursework there is even less excuse, because spelling checkers are always at hand.

Your turn. You will see a short passage full of apostrophe and spelling mistakes. Rewrite it correctly.
