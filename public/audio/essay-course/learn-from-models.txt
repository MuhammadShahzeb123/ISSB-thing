Lesson twelve. Learn from model essays.

The second half of the book is full of sample essays. They cover literature, poetry, media, history, novels, film, politics and critical thinking. The big lesson from all of them is simple. The same structure works in every subject. A clear opening, a structured development and a considered conclusion.

Each subject does add its own demands. History and politics need the facts at your fingertips, chosen with care. Literature needs close references and short quotations. Film needs you to describe scenes, because you cannot quote a picture. And every subject has its own proper terms, which you should use naturally.

The book also gives a checklist of questions an assessor asks while marking. Learn them, because you can ask them of your own essay. Does the opening deal with the topic and say something specific? Does it avoid empty waffle? Does each body paragraph have a key sentence? Does each deal mainly with one key point? Is that point backed up with evidence? Does the last sentence of each paragraph sum up or point ahead? Are the paragraphs linked, with a sense of flow? Does the final paragraph show that a conclusion is being reached, sum up the case, and bring the essay back to the question?

One chapter teaches critical thinking. Here you read someone else's argument and take it apart. You find the conclusion, the reasons given, and any counter arguments. Then you look for flaws in the reasoning, and for assumptions, things the writer takes for granted without proving them. This habit will help you think for yourself, which matters a great deal at the ISSB.

So when you read a good essay, do not just read it. Study it. Ask where the key sentences are, which linking words are used, what evidence is given, and how the conclusion is built.

Your turn. You will see a short sample paragraph. Name its key sentence, its linking word, the evidence it gives, and what its closing sentence does.
