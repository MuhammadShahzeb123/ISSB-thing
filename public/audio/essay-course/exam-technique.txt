Lesson fifteen. Writing under exam conditions.

The book has a striking idea. When you sit an exam, one of the things being tested is how good you are at sitting exams. Two students with the same knowledge can walk out with very different marks. The difference is how each one meets the demands of the exam.

The first golden rule is give the examiners what they want. Do not have a mental argument with the paper. Thoughts like this question is unfair will not help you. Just carry out the instructions to the letter.

Read the instructions with great care. If the paper says answer two questions from section A and one from section B, then do exactly that. Answering two from section B because you feel more confident there will simply cost you marks.

Next, divide your time sensibly. Look at the marks for each question. If one part is worth eight marks and another is worth fifteen, spend nearly twice as long on the second.

The book gives a clear example with four questions worth the same marks. One student spends most of the time on two favourite questions, then rushes the last two. Eighteen, eighteen, twelve and seven. That is fifty five. Another shares the time equally. Seventeen, seventeen, fifteen and fifteen. That is sixty four. Same knowledge, nine extra marks, just from managing time.

What if you run out of time and scribble the last answer in note form? The book warns that such answers are usually marked out of only half the marks. Notes are no replacement for a proper essay.

For your ISSB essay you get about thirty minutes for one essay. So use the method from this course. A few minutes to underline key words and plan. Most of your time for the body. And a couple of minutes at the end for a strong conclusion and a quick check of spelling and punctuation.

Your turn. Write a simple time plan for a thirty minute essay. Say how many minutes you will give to planning, the opening, the body, the conclusion and checking.
