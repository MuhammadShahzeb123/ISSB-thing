Pakistan cuts polio to 5 cases, but not zero.

On 29 September 2026, a laboratory in Islamabad confirmed sad news. A 17-month-old girl in Bannu district had polio.

She was Pakistan's 5th polio case of the year. Polio is a virus that can cause lifelong paralysis, the loss of movement in arms or legs. There is no cure, but vaccine drops can prevent it.

Pakistan and Afghanistan are the last 2 countries where wild polio has never been stopped. Pakistan is also growing fast.

The 2023 census, Pakistan's first digital census, counted 241.49 million people. The population was growing by 2.55 percent a year, the highest rate in the region. If that continues, it could double by 2050.

That means every year brings many new babies who need polio drops, and later a seat in school.

The progress is real. Pakistan had 74 polio cases in 2024 and 31 in 2025. So far in 2026, it has 5.

In the early 1990s, Pakistan had an estimated 20,000 cases a year. Vaccination has cut that by 99.8 percent.

Behind these numbers are ordinary men and women. From 21 September to 27 September 2026, more than 273,000 polio workers went door to door in 112 districts.

They gave drops to more than 28.2 million children under 5. Afghanistan ran its campaign in the same week. The World Health Organization treats the 2 countries as 1 shared area for the virus.

But the virus still hides in a few places. 4 of this year's 5 cases came from Bannu division in southern Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. The other was a 4-year-old child in Sujawal, Sindh.

Officials say poor security has stopped teams from reaching some Bannu children regularly since 2025. Global experts name southern Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Karachi as Pakistan's main polio hotspots.

There is also a missed deadline. Pakistan and Afghanistan aimed to stop the wild virus by June 2026. The Independent Monitoring Board, a panel that checks the global polio effort, says that target was missed.

Schools face a similar test. A federal education report released in February 2026 said 26.2 million children are out of school.

Like polio drops, education only works when it reaches every child, including girls and children in poor or unsafe areas.

Why does this matter to ordinary families? A single case can mean a child who never walks normally again. While the virus survives in any district, children everywhere remain at risk.

The mature view is that this is not only a health job. Polio teams need safety and respect. Parents need honest answers from doctors, teachers and religious scholars.

Pakistan has already come 99.8 percent of the way. The last part needs the same patience and courage, door after door.
