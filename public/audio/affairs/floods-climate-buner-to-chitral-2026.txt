From Buner to Chitral, floods teach hard lessons.

On the morning of 15 August 2025, Omar Khan left home for his shop in Buner. When he ran back, his house was gone. Only boulders were left.

The flood killed 24 members of his family. His wife was found still holding their baby son. Charities later helped build rooms in his family's new house.

That day a cloudburst hit Buner in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. A cloudburst is very heavy rain over a small area in a short time.

About 150 millimetres of rain fell in 1 hour. The provincial disaster authority says 256 people died in Buner alone.

In late August 2025, Punjab faced its own disaster. Heavy rain and water released from overflowing dams in India sent the Ravi, Sutlej and Chenab rivers over their banks.

A United Nations report called it Punjab's worst river flooding in nearly 4 decades. More than 4.7 million people were affected.

The 2025 monsoon killed 1,037 people across Pakistan. The National Disaster Management Authority, or NDMA, the main disaster agency, said about 3 million people were rescued or moved to safety.

It said the armed forces and Rescue 1122 played a crucial role, including rescues by air in the mountains.

The 2026 monsoon brought less rain. The Pakistan Meteorological Department, the weather office, said rain from 1 July to 30 September was 19.3 percent below normal. It linked this partly to El Niño, a warming of the Pacific Ocean that can weaken our monsoon.

Less rain did not mean safety. By 28 September 2026, NDMA had counted 199 deaths, including 91 children. Collapsing roofs and walls were a leading cause.

Rescue teams carried out 1,192 operations and rescued 10,684 people by 29 September 2026.

Heat brought its own danger. On 28 May 2026, Dadu in Sindh reached 51.5 degrees Celsius, a new record for the city.

Heat also melts mountain ice faster. In early August 2026, a glacial lake outburst flood and a cloudburst hit Chitral. A glacial lake outburst flood happens when a lake of melted ice suddenly bursts.

Bridges were washed away. Roads and internet links to the valley were cut.

Too little rain is also a risk. Sindh received 50.8 percent less rain than normal, and Karachi 92 percent less.

Why do floods still kill so many people? A World Bank survey of 1,207 households in Buner, Shangla and Swat found that 85 percent got no warning in 2025. The bank said Khyber Pakhtunkhwa had prepared mainly for river floods, not fast mountain floods.

Some help now comes from space. SUPARCO, Pakistan's space agency, uses satellite images to watch 131 glacial lake sites.

Dams can hold back floods and save water for dry years. Mohmand Dam on the Swat River was about 50 percent complete in January 2026. It is designed to control floods around Peshawar, Charsadda and Nowshera.

But Diamer-Bhasha Dam was less than 20 percent built by June 2025, against a target of almost 58 percent.

Some damage is man-made. In 2025, the Ravi flowed into housing societies built on its bed in Lahore. The climate change minister said rivers must be allowed to spread.

The sensible lesson is simple. We cannot stop the rain, but we can stop many deaths.

Warnings must reach every village. Dams must be finished on time, and nobody should build on a riverbed.

Rescuers will always be brave on the day of a flood. The real work must be done in the dry months before it.
