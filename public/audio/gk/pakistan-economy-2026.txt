Pakistan's economy in numbers.

Every June, a day before the budget, the Finance Minister stands up with a thick book. It is the Pakistan Economic Survey, the country's report card for the year. In June twenty twenty six, Muhammad Aurangzeb presented the survey for the year twenty twenty five to twenty six.

The headline was growth. The economy grew three point seven percent, up from about three point two percent the year before. The whole economy was worth about one hundred and twenty seven trillion rupees, or about four hundred and fifty two billion dollars.

Divide that by the people and you get income per person. It rose to one thousand nine hundred and one dollars. The rupee stayed steady at about two hundred and eighty to the dollar.

Farming grew almost three percent. Industry grew three and a half percent, pulled up by large factories, which grew about six percent. Services, the biggest part of the economy, grew about four percent.

Prices told a mixed story. Average inflation was about six percent. When world oil prices jumped during the Iran war, inflation in April touched almost eleven percent.

The government spent more carefully, and the budget deficit fell sharply in the first nine months.

Overseas Pakistanis kept the country afloat. They sent home more than thirty billion dollars in just nine months. Reserves stood at about twenty two billion dollars by the end of March.

But the debt is heavy. Total public debt reached about eighty three trillion rupees.

People matter most. The survey counts about two hundred and fifty two million Pakistanis. Literacy rose to sixty three percent. Unemployment rose to about seven percent. The monsoon floods of twenty twenty five killed more than a thousand people, a reminder that climate is part of the economy too.

So what does it all mean? Pakistan has steadied after coming close to default in twenty twenty three. Growth is back, but it is slow, debt is high, and too few people pay tax. Know these few numbers, and you can speak about the economy with confidence.
