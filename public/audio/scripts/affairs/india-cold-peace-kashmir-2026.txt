Kashmir, borders and a cold peace with India.

On 28 September 2026, Indian diplomat Alok Dimri attended a meeting in Islamabad. It was the first official Indian visit to Pakistan since the fighting of May 2025.

He came for the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation, or SCO, a regional group Pakistan now leads. "For me, this is the first opportunity to come to Pakistani soil," he told reporters.

Pakistan's Foreign Office called his visit "a welcome development". Indian sources said no separate India-Pakistan meeting was planned.

It stood out because ties are almost frozen. Since April 2025, both high commissions, or embassies, have been cut to 30 staff. Trade is suspended. Special South Asian visas were cancelled on both sides.

The skies are closed too. On 16 September 2026, Pakistan extended its ban on Indian aircraft until 24 October 2026. It was the 17th extension. India also keeps its airspace closed to Pakistani airlines.

India has kept the Kartarpur corridor shut since 7 May 2025. Sikh pilgrims used it to visit Gurdwara Darbar Sahib in Narowal without a visa. Pakistan says its side remains open.

On the Line of Control, the line that divides Kashmir, the ceasefire has mostly held.

At sea, it was different. On 15 September 2026, the Pakistani ship PNS Hunain and the Indian warship INS Kolkata collided in the Arabian Sea. Each side blamed the other. Pakistan said the Indian ship made aggressive moves near a Pakistani naval exercise. India said the Pakistani ship broke a 1991 agreement to keep warships apart.

On 1 October 2026, Pakistan's military spokesman, Lieutenant General Ahmed Sharif Chaudhry, presented a retired soldier, Muhammad Asif, to journalists. Indian forces and media had said a militant by that name was killed in Kashmir. "I was here, alive and healthy," Asif said.

On 2 October 2026, Indian border guards shot dead 2 Pakistani men near Kasur and injured a third. Pakistan says Azmat Rasool and Mohsin Jutt were unarmed villagers who walked a few steps past a border pillar. India says 3 men crossed into its territory and ignored warnings. On 3 October 2026, both countries summoned each other's diplomats.

Behind every incident sits Kashmir. Pakistan's position is that the dispute must be settled under UN Security Council resolutions, through a free vote of the Kashmiri people.

On 5 August 2026, Pakistan marked Youm-e-Istehsal, 7 years after India ended Kashmir's special status. Even Omar Abdullah, who became chief minister there through India's own election in 2024, spoke out. "We haven't forgotten, and we haven't reconciled," he said.

At the UN on 2 October 2026, an Indian delegate called Kashmir an "internal matter". A Pakistani diplomat replied that it is an internationally recognised disputed territory.

On 3 October 2026, India beat Pakistan by 19 runs in the Asian Games cricket final in Japan. Once again, the players did not shake hands.

This freeze has a human price. Fishermen sit in each other's jails. Pilgrims wait at a closed gate. Villagers near the border live with fear.

Pakistan can stand firm on Kashmir and its borders and still keep doors open. A collision at sea or a shot at the border can quickly grow into a crisis between 2 nuclear powers. Meetings like the SCO keep a channel alive.

Pakistan does not have to choose between dignity and dialogue. A strong and confident country can hold on to both.
