How the Gulf war reached Pakistani homes.

On 2 April 2026, the government announced the biggest one-day fuel price rise in Pakistan's history. Petrol jumped by 137 rupees to 458.40 rupees a litre. Diesel rose by 184 rupees to 520.35 rupees.

For a rickshaw driver or a truck owner, it was a heavy blow.

The cause was next door. On 28 February 2026, the United States and Israel attacked Iran.

Iran answered by blocking most ships in the Strait of Hormuz, a narrow sea route out of the Gulf. Before the war, about 20 percent of the world's oil and gas passed through it.

Most of Pakistan's imported LNG, or liquefied natural gas, comes from Qatar, inside the Gulf.

Prices had already risen once. On 6 March 2026, petrol went up by 55 rupees to 321.17 rupees.

After the April record, people were angry. The next day, the Prime Minister cut the petroleum levy, a fixed tax on each litre, by 80 rupees. Petrol fell to 378 rupees.

Since July, prices change every day with world prices. On 29 September 2026, petrol cost 389.03 rupees and diesel 404.97 rupees. Petrol is still about 46 percent above its pre-war price of 266.17 rupees.

Motorcycle and rickshaw users now get 500 rupees off petrol once a week. In its first 8 days, 4.7 million people used this relief scheme.

Gas was another worry. On 2 March 2026, Iranian drones hit Qatar's Ras Laffan gas complex. Qatar then paused deliveries under force majeure, a legal right to stop supply during events like war.

Pakistan normally gets about 9 Qatari LNG shiploads a month. By late September, it had received only 9 since March. Some areas faced power cuts of up to 12 hours.

Power plants burned more furnace oil instead. Its sales in September were 740 percent higher than a year earlier. Qatar has now extended its pause to early November.

Inflation, the speed at which prices rise, climbed too. It was 7.3 percent in March, 11.7 percent in May and 11.1 percent in August.

In September it eased to 10.3 percent. But electricity charges rose 15 percent in that month alone.

On 27 April 2026, the State Bank of Pakistan, the central bank, raised its policy rate to 11.5 percent. This key rate guides the cost of all loans. The bank held it there on 14 September 2026.

Circular debt is the chain of unpaid bills between power companies. It stood at 1,675 billion rupees at the end of June 2026.

Meanwhile, solar power has spread fast. Pakistan is now among the world's top new users of solar power. In February 2026, the regulator cut what it pays new solar users for power sent to the grid.

For ordinary people, energy is more than a bill. It is the bus fare, the price of flour carried by diesel trucks, and the urea fertilizer a farmer needs.

Gas is used to make urea, so a winter gas shortage could hurt crops.

Pakistan cannot end a war in the Gulf alone. In June 2026, it helped the US and Iran reach a temporary deal, the Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding.

Yet on 4 October 2026, Iran said the strait would stay closed until its conditions are met.

What Pakistan can control is its own need for imported fuel. More solar, hydropower and local gas would cut that need.

Honest prices, with help aimed only at the poor, would stop circular debt from growing.

Every rooftop panel and every paid bill makes the next shock a little smaller.

Points to remember.

The war began on 28 February 2026 and the Strait of Hormuz was largely blocked. Qatar paused LNG deliveries after an attack on Ras Laffan on 2 March 2026.
Petrol was 266.17 rupees before the war, hit a record 458.40 rupees on 2 April 2026, and was 389.03 rupees on 29 September 2026.
Inflation was 10.3 percent in September 2026, down from 11.1 percent in August.
The State Bank raised its policy rate to 11.5 percent on 27 April 2026 and held it on 14 September 2026.
Power-sector circular debt was 1,675 billion rupees at the end of June 2026.
