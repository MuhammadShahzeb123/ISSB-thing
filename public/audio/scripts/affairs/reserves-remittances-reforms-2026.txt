Record reserves, borrowed time and big reforms.

On 29 September 2026, Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif opened the day's trading at the London Stock Exchange. No Pakistani prime minister had done this before.

He was marking a 3 billion dollar Eurobond, a loan in dollars from investors abroad. Sold in early September, it was Pakistan's biggest ever bond sale abroad. Investors had offered nearly 6 billion dollars.

In April 2026, the picture was worrying. Pakistan and the United Arab Emirates did not agree to extend 3.5 billion dollars of UAE deposits. Such a failure had not happened in 7 years.

Pakistan repaid 3.45 billion dollars to the UAE by 23 April 2026. Saudi Arabia stepped in with a new 3 billion dollar deposit. It also extended an older 5 billion dollar deposit until 2028.

Today the numbers are much stronger. Reserves are the dollars a country keeps to pay for imports and debts.

On 25 September 2026, the central bank, the State Bank of Pakistan, held a record 21.44 billion dollars. With banks included, total reserves were 26.77 billion dollars.

On 2 October 2026, the rupee closed at 277.07 per dollar, slightly stronger than at the start of the year. On 24 August 2026, the rating agency Moody's raised Pakistan's credit grade to B3. That tells lenders the risk is lower than before.

Where do the dollars come from? The biggest single source is Pakistanis working abroad.

Their remittances, the money they send home, hit a record 41.6 billion dollars in the year to June 2026. In August 2026 alone, 3.66 billion dollars arrived, 16.5 percent more than a year before. Workers in Saudi Arabia and the UAE sent the most.

Exports are the weak point. Goods exports fell to 30.84 billion dollars in the year to June 2026, from 32.43 billion a year earlier.

In September 2026, exports rose 17.6 percent to 2.94 billion dollars. Yet they paid for only about 45 percent of that month's imports.

Foreign direct investment, money foreigners put into businesses here, fell to 1.64 billion dollars in the year to June 2026.

Some big reforms did move. On 29 June 2026, PIA, the national airline, passed into private hands. A group led by Arif Habib Corporation took control.

This is privatisation, selling a state company to private owners. The group had bid 135 billion rupees for 75 percent of PIA in December 2025.

Other plans slowed down. Reko Diq in Balochistan is among the world's largest untouched copper and gold deposits.

In early 2026, Barrick, the Canadian company building the mine, slowed work because of security risks. It will keep reviewing the project until mid-2027.

The Special Investment Facilitation Council was set up to speed up investment. It reports a pipeline of possible projects worth 40 billion dollars. But a pipeline is a list of plans, not money received.

Strong reserves mean a factory can import machines, and a family can buy medicine without panic. But the latest jump came mainly from the Eurobond, which is a loan. Its interest is 7.5 to 7.9 percent a year.

Loans and friendly deposits buy Pakistan time. They do not build its future.

That needs more exports and more foreign investment. It also needs reforms that succeed.

A PIA that flies on time would send a strong signal. So would a safe, working Reko Diq.

Record reserves give Pakistan a chance. The real test is to earn dollars, not just borrow them.
