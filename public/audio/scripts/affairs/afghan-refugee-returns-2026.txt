Afghan families leave the only home they know.

On 13 July 2026, Zia Rahman waited with his family at a transit centre near the Torkham border. He is 36 years old, and he was born in Pakistan.

"Neither my children, nor those of my brothers ever visited Afghanistan," he told a reporter. Now they had to move there. A local volunteer said 400 to 600 families were crossing at Torkham every day.

3 days earlier, a new deadline had passed. From 10 July 2026, police were ordered to arrest any Afghan without a valid visa.

Pakistan has hosted Afghans since the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in 1979. Before 2021, about 1.5 million registered Afghans lived here. More than 500,000 more came after the Taliban took Kabul.

In September 2023, the government approved the Illegal Foreigners Repatriation Plan. Repatriation means sending people back to their own country. At first, it targeted Afghans with no papers.

In 2025, the plan grew. Holders of Afghan Citizen Cards, another ID document, had to leave by 31 March 2025. Then the Proof of Registration cards, or PoR cards, expired on 30 June 2025.

For years, PoR cards had given refugees legal stay. Their holders were told to leave by 1 September 2025.

The interior ministry told a Senate committee that 1,155,221 Afghans were repatriated in 2025. The UN refugee agency, UNHCR, counted about 1 million returns and deportations from Pakistan that year.

The flow has not stopped. UNHCR counted 705,400 more returns in 2026 by 12 September. The UN migration agency, IOM, says 2,722,091 Afghans have gone back since September 2023.

In IOM's September survey, 95 percent of families said fear of arrest was their main reason for leaving.

Pakistan's reasons are serious. Officials say militants use Afghan soil, and some Afghan nationals have been linked to attacks. They also point to the cost of hosting millions of people for over 40 years.

The government says the rules apply to all foreigners living here illegally. In Peshawar, police were warned of strict action if they harassed Afghan families or demanded money.

Aid groups worry about the human cost. UNHCR has asked Pakistan not to send back people who need protection, especially women and girls.

Students feel it too. On 1 September 2026, the medical council PMDC told Afghan medical students to leave. Nilofar Nikzad, whose studies in Afghanistan stopped under Taliban limits on women's education, joined 12 others in court.

On 11 September 2026, the Lahore High Court halted the order. The Foreign Office said Afghan students with valid visas can stay and finish their degrees.

A country has the right to know who lives inside it. In a time of attacks, security cannot be ignored. But the UN says many returnees were born and raised outside Afghanistan.

The wise path is firm and fair. Remove criminals and check papers, but offer legal visas to students, traders and people at special risk.

Pakistan can be proud of hosting Afghans for more than 40 years. How this period ends will also be remembered, in Kabul and at home.
