The Indus Waters Treaty still stands, says Hague court.

On 31 August 2026, a court in The Hague gave a clear answer. The Indus Waters Treaty "remains fully in force", it said. All 5 members agreed.

The treaty was signed in Karachi on 19 September 1960, with help from the World Bank. India got the 3 eastern rivers, the Ravi, Beas and Sutlej. Pakistan got most of the 3 western rivers, the Indus, Jhelum and Chenab.

These rivers water about 80 percent of Pakistan's irrigated farms, Reuters reported. Wheat, rice and sugarcane depend on them.

On 23 April 2025, a day after the Pahalgam attack, India said it would hold the treaty "in abeyance", which means on hold. It said this would last until Pakistan ends support for cross-border terrorism.

Pakistan's National Security Committee replied the next day. It said the treaty has no rule that lets either side stop it alone. Any attempt to stop Pakistan's water or turn it away, it warned, "will be considered as an Act of War".

Pakistan chose the law. Since 2016, it had challenged the design of 2 Indian dams built to make electricity, Kishenganga and Ratle. A Court of Arbitration, a special court set up under the treaty, heard the case. A Neutral Expert, an engineer named by the World Bank, studied technical points.

India refused to join the court, but the court moved ahead. On 27 June 2025, it ruled that India's decision could not remove its power to hear the case. On 8 August 2025, it said India must "let flow" the western rivers for Pakistan to use freely.

India also quit the Neutral Expert's process on 28 July 2025. The expert carried on without India.

On 15 May 2026, in another award, or decision, the court set limits on "pondage". This is the water a river dam may hold back for daily power needs.

Then came 31 August 2026. The court checked the reasons India had given in public, such as terrorism, population change and climate change. None of them, it found, could justify suspending the treaty.

The court said it took India's terrorism claims "extremely seriously". But the treaty is about water, not terrorism.

A separate order stops India from pouring concrete above certain levels on the Ratle dam wall. This lasts until 90 days after the Neutral Expert's decision, expected around July 2027.

India rejected this ruling too, on the same day. Its foreign ministry called the court "illegally constituted". It said India's decision to hold the treaty in abeyance "remains in force".

Foreign Minister Ishaq Dar welcomed the award. On 1 October 2026, the Foreign Office said India "cannot escape from the binding awards" through its own statements.

At the UN on 25 September 2026, Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif repeated the warning. Any attempt to stop, slow or turn away Pakistan's share, he said, "will be treated as an act of war".

For a farmer in Punjab or Sindh, this is not a lawyers' game. Water held back at the wrong time can ruin a crop. India still sends flood warnings, but only through diplomats.

Pakistan did not answer a broken promise by breaking the treaty. It went to the treaty's own courts and kept winning. A court cannot force open a dam gate, but it shows the world who follows the rules.

The law has spoken. The treaty still binds both countries.

Points to remember.

The Indus Waters Treaty was signed in Karachi on 19 September 1960. The western rivers, Indus, Jhelum and Chenab, go mainly to Pakistan.
India put the treaty "in abeyance" on 23 April 2025. Pakistan said stopping its water would be an act of war.
Court of Arbitration rulings came on 27 June 2025, 8 August 2025, 15 May 2026 and 31 August 2026.
On 31 August 2026 the court ruled unanimously that the treaty "remains fully in force".
India calls the court "illegally constituted" and says its abeyance remains in force.
