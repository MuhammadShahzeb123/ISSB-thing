Trump's tariff wall, the courts and the voters.

On 20 February 2026, the US Supreme Court handed President Donald Trump a major defeat. By 6 votes to 3, the judges ruled that an emergency law did not allow him to impose tariffs.

A tariff is a tax on goods that come from abroad. Under the US Constitution, the power to tax belongs to Congress, the American parliament.

Trump had used that emergency law since 2025 to tax imports from most countries. In April 2025, US tariffs on Chinese goods reached 145 percent. India later faced 50 percent. Pakistan got 19 percent after a deal in July 2025.

The ruling forced Washington to repay importers. But Trump moved fast. He used another law to put a 10 percent tariff on imports from almost everywhere for 150 days. When it expired on 24 July 2026, new tariffs took its place.

The new tariffs use Section 301, a US trade law against unfair practices. Washington said 60 economies were not doing enough to keep out goods made with forced labour. It taxed their goods at 10 or 12.5 percent.

Pakistan and India both landed in the 10 percent group. So the edge Pakistan had over India in 2025 is gone. Analysts estimate the tax could cost Pakistan's textile exporters about 564 million dollars this financial year.

India faces another risk. When Gulf supplies fell during the Iran war, Russia supplied nearly half of India's oil imports by July. A new US law now allows tariffs of up to 100 percent on the biggest buyers of Russian oil.

More tariffs may come. A second US probe is looking at 16 trading partners, including China, Japan and the European Union.

The election is only weeks away. On 3 November 2026, Americans will choose all 435 members of the House of Representatives and 35 of the 100 senators.

The mood is sour. A Reuters and Ipsos poll in September put Trump's approval at 32 percent, the lowest of his career. Only 17 percent approved of how he handles the cost of living. Most voters blame the Iran war for higher petrol prices, one poll found.

Forecasters say Democrats are favoured to win the House. The Senate is closer. A Democratic House could investigate the government and slow Trump's plans.

What does this mean for Pakistan? Warm words help. At the UN in September, Trump called Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif a "terrific man". Yet Pakistani garments and home textiles still pay the new 10 percent tax.

The mature lesson is to build strength that does not depend on one leader's mood. That means cheaper energy, real checks against forced labour and more buyers in Europe, the Gulf, China and Central Asia.

In Washington, friendship can open a door. But prices, rules and voters decide who stays inside.
