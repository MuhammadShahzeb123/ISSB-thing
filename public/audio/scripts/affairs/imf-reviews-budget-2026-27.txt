IMF talks and a budget built on discipline.

On 29 September 2026, Finance Minister Muhammad Aurangzeb held the opening meeting with a team from the International Monetary Fund, or IMF. Led by Iva Petrova, the team had come to check Pakistan's loan for the 4th time.

The IMF is a global lender. It helps countries short of dollars, but asks for reforms in return.

On 25 September 2024, it approved a 37-month loan of about 7 billion dollars. This is the Extended Fund Facility.

In May 2025, it added about 1.4 billion dollars to help Pakistan face climate disasters. This is the Resilience and Sustainability Facility.

The money comes in parts, after a review every 6 months. The IMF board approved the first 3 reviews on 9 May 2025, 8 December 2025 and 8 May 2026. By then, Pakistan had received about 4.8 billion dollars from both loans.

The conditions are strict. Pakistan must collect more tax from more people.

It must keep a primary surplus, which means earning more than it spends before paying interest. Fuel, gas and electricity must be sold at their real cost.

Help for the poor should go to them directly, through the Benazir Income Support Programme, or BISP.

The year to June 2026 showed discipline. The fiscal deficit, the gap between government spending and income, fell to 2.6 percent of GDP. GDP is the value of everything the country produces.

Officials called it the lowest deficit in 22 years. The economy grew by about 3.7 percent.

But the Federal Board of Revenue, or FBR, the national tax collector, raised about 13 trillion rupees. That was over 1 trillion rupees below its first target.

On 12 June 2026, the government presented a budget of 18.77 trillion rupees. Interest on old loans takes 8.05 trillion rupees, more than 42 percent of it.

Defence gets 3 trillion rupees, 17.6 percent more than last year's original amount. Security threats on both borders lie behind the rise.

There was relief too. Government salaries and pensions rose by 7 percent. Income tax rates fell for salaried people in 4 income slabs.

Money for BISP rose 17 percent to 838 billion rupees. The FBR must now collect 15.26 trillion rupees this year. The National Assembly passed the budget on 23 June 2026.

By 3 October 2026, the IMF had shared its first draft of new targets. It was worried that circular debt reached 1,675 billion rupees at the end of June, above the agreed limit.

Circular debt means unpaid bills piling up in the power sector.

Both sides also disagreed over a fuel relief scheme for motorcyclists and owners of small cars. The IMF wants such help to go only through a targeted system like BISP.

On the good side, the FBR beat its tax target for July to September. If talks succeed and the IMF board agrees, about 1.2 billion dollars could follow.

For a family in Multan, this matters. IMF approval gives other lenders confidence. But every loan must be repaid, and interest already takes the biggest share of the budget.

This is Pakistan's 25th IMF programme since 1958. The IMF is neither a friend nor an enemy. It lends money and asks for discipline in return.

Some reforms are late, and the tax base is still narrow. By 30 September 2026, only 4 new shopkeepers had filed returns under a simple fixed tax scheme.

Fair taxes, efficient state companies and help aimed at the truly poor can make this the last programme.

A country that pays its own bills is free to make its own choices.

Points to remember.

The IMF approved a 37-month Extended Fund Facility of about 7 billion dollars on 25 September 2024, and a climate loan of about 1.4 billion dollars on 9 May 2025.
The IMF board approved reviews on 9 May 2025, 8 December 2025 and 8 May 2026. About 4.8 billion dollars has been received.
Talks on the 4th review began in late September 2026. About 1.2 billion dollars is at stake.
In 2025 to 26 the fiscal deficit fell to 2.6 percent of GDP and the economy grew about 3.7 percent.
Budget 2026 to 27 is 18.77 trillion rupees. Interest takes 8.05 trillion, defence gets 3 trillion and BISP gets 838 billion.
