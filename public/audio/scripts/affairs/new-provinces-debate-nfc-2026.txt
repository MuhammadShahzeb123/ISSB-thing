The new provinces debate tests Pakistan's federation.

On 3 September 2026, the Sindh Assembly passed a firm resolution, or formal statement. "Sindh is one and shall remain one," it declared. It rejected any plan to divide the province or put any part of it under federal control.

Members present all voted for it, after the opposition MQM-P and Jamaat-i-Islami walked out.

Pakistan has 4 provinces, plus the federal capital, Azad Jammu and Kashmir, and Gilgit-Baltistan. Demands for new provinces, such as South Punjab, Bahawalpur and Hazara, are decades old.

Changing a province's borders is hard by design. It needs a constitutional amendment backed by at least 2 of every 3 members in both houses of Parliament. Under Article 239(4), that province's own assembly must also approve it with the same majority.

On 30 July 2026, Interior Minister Mohsin Naqvi told an economic summit that "the system we are living under has collapsed". He urged all parties to sit together and settle the issue.

The next day, the army's spokesman, Lieutenant General Ahmed Sharif Chaudhry, said any "administrative reset" must follow constitutional and legal methods and the will of the people.

In early September, Planning Minister Ahsan Iqbal suggested 12 to 15 provinces, made by splitting each province into 3. He said this would need broad political agreement and a fair balance of Senate seats.

The Pakistan Peoples Party, or PPP, rules Sindh and is a key partner of the PML-N in the federal coalition, or ruling alliance. It says any new province must follow the Constitution and have broad agreement. Sindh Chief Minister Murad Ali Shah said there would be "no compromise" on Sindh's unity.

PTI chairman Gohar Ali Khan also wants agreement first, through a commission.

The PML-N has not yet taken a final position. Rana Sanaullah, the Prime Minister's political adviser, says Parliament must decide and no bill exists. Both houses are expected to debate the issue in October.

Many link this to talk of a 28th Amendment. The government has not presented any such bill in Parliament. In May, Law Minister Azam Nazeer Tarar said no final draft existed.

Money is the other big argument. The National Finance Commission, or NFC, award is the formula for sharing federal taxes. Under the 2009 award, provinces get 57.5 percent of this money.

The centre is unhappy with its 42.5 percent, saying it cannot cover debt payments and defence. According to the Chief Minister of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, the Prime Minister promised on 10 June 2026 to update the award within 180 days.

For ordinary people, this is about schools, hospitals and police. Supporters say smaller provinces would bring government closer to citizens. Critics warn that Pakistan's debt and limited resources cannot carry the cost.

The wise path is the constitutional one. Any new province should come through Parliament and the consent of the people and assemblies concerned.

Stronger local governments could also help. Punjab has not held local elections since 2015. They are now planned, in principle, for 15 December 2026 to 15 January 2027.

Provinces exist to serve citizens, not the other way round. Whatever Parliament decides, every Sindhi, Punjabi, Pashtun, Baloch and every other Pakistani must feel the country belongs equally to them.

Points to remember.

The Sindh Assembly resolution of 3 September 2026 said "Sindh is one and shall remain one".
Article 239(4) says the affected provincial assembly must approve any change to its borders by a two-thirds vote.
Ahsan Iqbal suggested 12 to 15 provinces in early September 2026. No 28th Amendment bill has been tabled.
Under the 2009 NFC award, provinces get 57.5 percent of the divisible pool and the centre gets 42.5 percent.
Punjab local elections are planned for 15 December 2026 to 15 January 2027. The last were held in 2015.
